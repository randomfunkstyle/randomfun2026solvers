How memory works

A 100-cell RAM with no memory. The tape is a pipe — 107 cells of it, holding 100 values that circulate forever — and a single little man walks the whole loop on every single operation. That one fact is the machine's entire cost structure.

Measured on the reference interpreter (littleman/examples/memory2.man). Scoring is footprint-tick: max(w,h)² × avg_ticks, so only the larger dimension is billed, and it is squared.

1. Four rooms and two very long pipes

A little man may never leave his room, so all the work happens in one 24×25 compute room. The tape can't loop back into that room directly — SPEC lists "a pipe looping back to its own room" as a load error — so a 6×5 relay room exists for no reason other than to turn the ring around.

2. The tape is made of pipe

A pipe is a FIFO whose capacity is its length: each cell holds one value and shifts one cell per tick. So 107 cells of pipe = 107 slots, of which 100 hold the memory. Nothing stores the address — position in the queue is the address.

4. Three tricks worth stealing

The sign of A carries the opcode

After decoding, READ leaves A = 100 − addr and WRITE leaves A = addr − 100 — the same magnitude, opposite signs. Since addr < 100, neither is ever zero. The value is parked in B across the whole seek loop, then one W brings it back and one X branches on its sign. So the opcode survives a hundred rotations without a register, a flag, or a second literal.

S emits and preserves in one glyph

A READ has to hand the value to the output and keep it on the tape. S sends A into every outgoing pipe at once — output pipe and tape ring together — so the answer is emitted and the cell is rewritten by a single instruction. No copy, no second pass.

The counted loop zigzags, so both directions do work

A man needs a closed walking cycle, and the smallest cycle holding two operations is 3×2 = 6 cells. This machine unrolls it across two columns: down the right doing r s m, up the left doing r s m, with a d test at each end. Every leg rotates one value instead of every other leg, so a rotation costs ~6.6 ticks rather than ~12.

5. Where the 13,955 ticks go

Two costs, and neither is the arithmetic. Setup: 100 zeros pushed into the ring at 8 ticks each, so nothing can be served for the first 855 ticks. Per operation: seek addr values, do the read or write, then restore 99 − addr — exactly 100 rotations, whatever the address.

Ticks per operation

Converges on ~635 — one full 100-value lap. An op at address 0 costs 660 ticks; address 99 adds only 548 more, because the lap is walked either way.

Share of runtime spent on setup

The 855-tick fill is 94% of the smallest case and 1% of the largest. Because the score averages over cases, it is 6.1% of the total — and it is the same 855 ticks seven times.

6. What that says about making it faster

The full lap is structural, not sloppy: a pipe cannot skip, so reaching cell k means physically moving every value in front of it. Three ways to break it, with measured or estimated payoffs:

changemechanismpayoff
Lazy rotationTrack the ring's offset and rotate (addr − offset) mod 100 instead of a full lap. Mean forward delta on the dominant case is 50.0, so the lap halves. Needs no new rooms — the offset can ride the ring as a header word.~1.8×
Parallel ringsTen rings of ten, so a seek moves ≤10 values. Selection is free: pipe binding is positional, proven by two-roms.man. Costs one relay, not ten — a data ring is permanently full, so its r never blocks.~3.5×
Compact redrawThe 31×32 box is 65% blank, with a 7×10 empty rectangle inside the compute room. Pure routing slack; footprint is squared.~1.8×
