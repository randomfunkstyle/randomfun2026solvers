# Four days inside a 2D grid — ICFP Contest 2026

Team **randomfunkstyle**. 16 problems, every public and private case passing.
An illustrated version of this page, with the timeline and one decision tree per
problem: **[`writeup.html`](writeup.html)** (open it locally; it is one
self-contained file, generated by
[`littleman/tools/make_writeup_html.py`](littleman/tools/make_writeup_html.py)).

## The task, in three sentences

Programs are written in **littleman**: a 2D ASCII grid where little men walk from
cell to cell, each holding two values, sending and receiving through pipes that
are FIFOs whose capacity is their length. Every operation is a glyph the man
steps on, and a man walking into a wall ends the program. The score is
`max(width, height)² × average ticks`, lower better — so you are charged for the
box you occupy and for the time you take, and the box is squared.

Spec as we reconstructed it: [`littleman/SPEC.md`](littleman/SPEC.md).
Scoring rules: [`littleman/GRADING.md`](littleman/GRADING.md).

## The strategy, and why it was two strategies

Points are `passing cases / total cases` **plus** `teams you beat on score`. Those
two halves want opposite machines, so we built both, in this order:

1. **LM-1 — a CPU written in littleman, and a compiler that targets it.**
   One machine, sixteen programs. It scores badly *on purpose*: a CPU is large
   and slow. It banks the test-case half of every problem's points, and it does
   it by day two. ([`littleman/ARCH.md`](littleman/ARCH.md))
2. **Hand-built dataflow grids** for the problems where the ranking half was
   still worth having. A walked glyph costs **1 tick**; an issued LM-1
   instruction costs **46**. That ratio, measured on day two
   ([`littleman/DATAFLOW-SURVEY.md`](littleman/DATAFLOW-SURVEY.md)), is the whole
   second half of the contest.

The compiler earned the coverage. The hand-built grids earned up to **3,457×** on
the score.

## Timeline

**Before the contest (20–22 Jul).** A batch/interactive solver harness: one
`./solve` entrypoint, JSON envelopes in and out, a per-run worktree, an
interactive stdio protocol for chatty problems. Written against a *guess* about
the contest shape, and the guess was wrong — 2026 was "write a program in an
esolang", with no server-side solver loop at all. What survived was the
repository and the habit of scripting everything.

**Day 1 (Fri 24 Jul, 12:00 →).** Reference tooling and primitives in parallel: a
headless `.man` runner over the bundled wasm engine, then a circulating store, a
ring, a four-pipe CPU, a Z3 pipe router. **22:04** — `memory` solved on a
rotating pipe-tape, our first accepted solution. **22:31** — a *generated* CPU
runs `triangle`. **23:04** — the call that shaped everything after it: synthesise
a **per-program** CPU, not one universal one, so a program only pays for the
opcodes it uses.

**Day 2 (25 Jul).** LM-1 becomes a real toolchain — ISA table, assembler,
emulator, a looping ROM, a display block for the LM-75 panel. `brackets` and
`tcp` at 02:26, `sudoku-validity` at 03:35, `plotter` at 04:19, `gradebook`
right after. **09:39** a submission tool with a score-named archive; **10:38**
all ten solved problems judge-verified. Then the profiler, and the discovery
that most of our cost model was wrong (below). **15:00** the dataflow survey.
**19:28** `tcp` rebuilt as a 17-word ring machine: **168×** better than the
compiled version.

**Day 3 (26 Jul).** Both tracks at full speed. Compiler track: the
`little-little-man` interpreter (a littleman interpreter written for our CPU
written in littleman), `pathfinder`'s bit-parallel BFS, `matmul`'s STREAM tier,
`snake`'s coprocessor. Hand-built track: `brackets` 25×25, `sudoku-validity`
20×20, `memory`'s one-pass ring, `history-lesson` at 90×90.

**Day 4 (27 Jul, to the deadline).** Hand-built grids everywhere — `tcp` 17×17,
`sort-numbers` and `reverse-a-list` at 14×14, `subset-sum` split into two rooms
at 80×84, `matmul` dense at 72×81 — and the last submission of the contest, a
ROM re-fold on `little-little-man` worth 13%. Somebody also got a first-person
raycaster running on the LM-1 CPU, which is not worth any points at all.

## What we tried, per problem

`✓` shipped or measured win · `✗` measured dead end · `·` step or parked idea.
The trees are drawn properly in [`writeup.html`](writeup.html); this is the same
content in text.

**little-little-man** — interpret a littleman program.
`·` needs a real store → `✓` interpreter on the LM-1 CPU → `✗` the tape ring
capped at 107 slots, we needed 427 → `✓` serpentine tape ring at zero width cost
→ `✗` packing 4 cells per word (the read-modify-write costs more than the shorter
tape saves) → `✓` bank the store, hot man-memory beside the tape, **2.36×** →
`✓` four-word tape skips → `✗` rolling the three men into one loop
(score-neutral) → `·` 56% of ticks turned out to be the CPU waiting out a ROM lap
→ `✗` a faster discard drain alone bought 0.18% → `✓` buffer the ROM corridor,
because a pipe *is* a FIFO → `✓` re-fold the ROM against the machine's real
bounding box (−13%) → `·` a two-bank ring repeater at 1 word/tick, designed, not
built. ([`littleman/LLM-DESIGN.md`](littleman/LLM-DESIGN.md),
[`littleman/ROM-RECIRCULATION.md`](littleman/ROM-RECIRCULATION.md))

**tcp** — `·` compiled program at 112×78 → `✓` compaction and ROM packing (−21%)
→ `✓` rebuild as a 17-word ring machine (**168×**) → `✓` two-tape queue, 17×17.

**brackets** — `✗` a pipe cannot be a stack (pop-newest is O(depth)) → `·` stack
in the tape → `·` stack threaded through a ring's data cells → `✓` the whole
stack packed into one integer with `/` → `✗` a 25×14 attempt that passed 16/26.

**sudoku-validity** — `·` 27 unit masks on the CPU, one lap per cell → `✓`
hand-built 20×20 ring → `✗` bit-packing and a systolic variant, both measured,
both lose.

**plotter** — `✗` the compiled machine was 6% *over* the step cap on the worst
legal case → `✓` cut tape accesses per pixel → `✓` replace the CPU entirely with
a line-drawing block. ([`littleman/PLOTTER-BLOCK.md`](littleman/PLOTTER-BLOCK.md))

**gradebook** — `✗` the tape's cost was modelled per slot; it is per *access* →
`✓` order the CPU's lanes by how often each opcode runs → `✓` move the output
room off the binding axis → `✓` one-pass ring worker at 70×70.

**matmul** — `·` correct compiled program, but every element is a tape access →
`✓` STREAM, a third memory tier of rotate-only rings with a fused MAC → `✓` a
dense hand-built band machine → `✗` the MAC rectangle and a one-row-per-pipe band
are mutually exclusive → `✗` a stall that took four ruled-out causes and two
phantom pipe mouths to find → `·` a systolic MAC chain, validated one stage deep.

**snake** — `·` compiled display machine at 123×129 → `✓` move the body into a
*write-only* coprocessor ring (4.7×) → `✓` a coprocessor may own the display →
`✓` hand-built 75×69 with fused ring laps → `·` 8.8e7 built locally after the
last submission window closed.

**pathfinder** — `✗` bitplane BFS, one word per row: 17/18 → `✓` bit-parallel BFS
on four 64-bit words: 18/18 → `✗` the cross-band placer wedges on real floorplans
→ `✓` band-grid layout with anchors derived from the loop order → `·` a
paint-only coprocessor variant, scaffolded, never finished.

**subset-sum** — `✓` the survey said it was unlockable with two registers → `·`
one-room ring at 81×253, where rows turned out to be the scarce dimension → `✓`
turn every ring rotation around (92×153 → 92×128) → `✓` split into two rooms on
one ring, 80×84.

**memory** — `✓` rotating pipe-tape, the contest's first accepted solution → `·`
a 31×31 ring that was small but slow → `✗` a 55×308 one-pass worker that was fast
but enormous → `✓` fold the one-pass worker into 108×107.

**sort-numbers + reverse-a-list** — `·` 25×25 and 19×18 rings straight off the
primitives → `✓` feed the ring from the relay (17×17 → 14×14) → `✓` a pair ring
carrying two values a lap, 13×13 → `✗` a counterpipe variant, 2× worse, archived
anyway → `✓` one loop test per lane instead of per value.

**little-little-little-man** — an interpreter for our interpreter. `✗` packing 8
classes per word needs three live values and a man has two hands → `✓` one class
per ring word, so the decode body holds nothing across a send → `✓` hold the
word, not the lap (5.6e10 → 7.4e9).

**history-lesson** — `✓` a rule-respecting ROM snake packer for 2,810 fixed
tokens → `✓` noticing that this problem is scored on footprint *alone*, so all
the tick work everywhere else was irrelevant here.

**triangle** — `✓` hand-drawn 8×8 in the first hours, never beaten. It was the
proof that bespoke beats generated, a day before we could measure why.

## Ideas that generalise

1. **A walked glyph is 1 tick; a CPU instruction is 46.** Compile for coverage,
   hand-build for rank. Knowing the ratio is what let us stop optimising the
   compiler and start replacing it.
2. **The short side of the box is free.** `max(w,h)²` charges one dimension, so
   narrowing an already-narrow machine is worth exactly zero — and a row of the
   *binding* dimension is never worth spending to shorten a pipe.
3. **Pipes are the memory.** Capacity equals length, so a long corridor is a
   queue you have already paid for. A ring needs `payload + 1` cells and
   deadlocks **silently** below that. A pipe cannot be a stack: pack the stack
   into an integer instead (`brackets`, 9,604 → 625 of area).
4. **Every ring costs a turnaround room** — ~6 ticks a word no matter how fast
   the worker is. Measure the *producer* before optimising the consumer: on
   `little-little-man` a 1.6× faster drain bought 0.18%, because the ROM man set
   the pace all along.
5. **Nearest-pipe binding is geometry, not readiness.** A room that moves reads
   the wrong pipe with no error at all — the failure mode is a wrong answer, so
   every layout move is followed by a binding check.
6. **Re-measure every inherited constant.** Ring rotation cost was 1.9×
   optimistic, the tape's cost was per-access and not per-slot, and `plotter`
   shipped 6% over the step cap on a figure nobody had run. Three of our largest
   wins were corrections to numbers we had assumed.
7. **Build the verifier you can afford to run.** The bundled wasm engine OOMs on
   our largest machines; a native tick loop
   ([`fast_littleman.py`](solvers/python/randomfun2026solvers/fast_littleman.py))
   made a 14-case sweep a 90-second routine, and that is what made sweeping
   parameters possible at all.
8. **Make the submission tool unable to lose work.** Every graded grid is
   archived under its server-verified score, so a listing sorts best-first and a
   worse run can never overwrite a better one.

## What we would do differently

- **Start the hand-built track on day one for the small problems.** `triangle`
  was hand-drawn in the first hours and never beaten; the same instinct applied
  to `tcp` a day earlier was worth thousands of times its score.
- **Keep one source of truth for each machine's real bounding box.** The last win
  of the contest was entirely a stale constant that priced the height of a
  machine which had since gotten shorter.
- **Submit earlier and more often.** The judge's tick average ran a consistent
  **1.098×** our local one. We only established that on the final day; until then
  every decision carried an error bar it did not need.
