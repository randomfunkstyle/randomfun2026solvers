Four days inside a 2D grid

ICFP Contest 2026 · team randomfunkstyle · 16 problems solved, every public and private case passing · the repository

The task was to write programs in littleman: a 2D ASCII language where little men walk a grid of rooms, carry two values in their hands, and talk through pipes that are FIFOs whose capacity is their length. Score is max(width, height)² × average ticks, lower better, so a program is charged for the box it occupies and the time it runs.

We took two runs at it, in this order and on purpose: build a CPU and compile to it so that every problem is solved and every test-case point is banked, then hand-build dataflow grids for the problems where the ranking half of the points was still on the table. The compiler earned the coverage; the hand-built grids earned up to 3,457× on the score.

How it went

Three tracks ran in parallel for most of the contest, in separate git worktrees with agents driving each one. The pre-contest days are on their own strip: they were spent on a batch/interactive solver harness for a contest that turned out not to work that way at all.

Ideas that generalise

  1. A walked glyph costs 1 tick; an issued CPU instruction costs 46. That single ratio, measured on day two (DATAFLOW-SURVEY.md), decided the second half of the contest. A compiler buys correctness; only hand-built dataflow buys rank.
  2. The short side of the box is free. Score charges max(w,h)², so narrowing an already-narrow machine is worth exactly zero, and a row of the binding dimension is never worth spending to shorten a pipe.
  3. Pipes are the memory. Capacity equals length, so a corridor is a queue you already paid for; a ring needs payload + 1 cells and deadlocks silently below that. A pipe cannot be a stack — pack the stack into an integer instead, which is what took brackets from 9,604 to 625 area.
  4. Every ring costs a turnaround room — about 6 ticks a word, whatever the worker does. Measure the producer before optimising the consumer: on little-little-man a 1.6× faster drain bought 0.18%, because the ROM man was the one setting the pace.
  5. Nearest-pipe binding is geometry, not readiness. A misplaced room reads the wrong pipe with no error at all, so every layout move is followed by a binding check.
  6. Re-measure every inherited constant. Ring rotation cost was 1.9× optimistic, the tape's cost was per-access and not per-slot, and plotter shipped 6% over the step cap on a number nobody had run. Three of our biggest wins were just correcting a number we had assumed.
  7. Build the verifier you can afford to run. The bundled wasm engine OOMs on our largest machines, so a native tick loop was worth more than any single optimisation — it made a 90-second sweep of 14 cases routine.
  8. Make the submission tool unable to lose work. Every graded grid is archived under its server-verified score, so a listing sorts best-first and a worse run can never overwrite a better one.

What we would do differently

  • Start the hand-built track on day one for the small problems. triangle was hand-drawn in the first hours and never beaten; the same instinct applied to tcp a day earlier would have been worth thousands of times its score.
  • Keep a single source of truth for each machine's real bounding box. The last win of the contest (−13% on little-little-man) was entirely a stale constant that had priced the height of a machine that had since gotten shorter.
  • Submit earlier and more often. The judge's tick average ran a consistent 1.098× our local one; we only established that on the final day, and until then every decision carried an unnecessary error bar.
